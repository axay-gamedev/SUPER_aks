What is superconductivity?

Superconductivity is a state in which a material exhibits zero electrical resistance and expels magnetic flux from its interior (the Meissner effect), when cooled below its critical temperature under suitable conditions.

02

Critical temperature · Tc

The transition temperature below which a material becomes superconducting. It is measured in kelvin (K).

03

Critical magnetic field

The maximum magnetic field a superconducting state can tolerate. Type-I materials have one critical field; Type-II materials have lower and upper critical fields (Hc1 and Hc2).

04

Type-I materials

Usually pure elemental metals. They show an abrupt transition to the normal state at one thermodynamic critical field and have relatively low field tolerance.

05

Type-II materials

Often alloys and compounds. Between Hc1 and Hc2, magnetic flux enters in quantized vortices, allowing superconductivity to persist in much stronger fields.

Why does it matter?

Superconductors are used in MRI and NMR magnets, particle accelerators, sensitive magnetic-field sensors, research magnets and emerging power technologies.
